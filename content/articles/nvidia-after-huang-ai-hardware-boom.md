# Nvidia After Huang: The Bill Comes Due for the AI Hardware Boom

For 33 years, Jensen Huang was Nvidia's most effective product. He sold the roadmap, the ecosystem, and the idea that accelerating computing was a civilisational project rather than a quarterly business. On 12 November he resigned as chief executive, and Nvidia named chief financial officer Colette Kress as interim CEO.

The immediate cause was a personal scandal. Huang acknowledged an affair with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a company that has been investigated over the diversion of restricted chips. She has also been alleged to be an agent of China's intelligence service. TechCrunch reported that Nvidia's potential corporate liability for export-control violations likely factored into the decision to part ways with its founder.

It is tempting to treat the story as a governance melodrama. That would miss the point. Huang's departure lands on top of two structural problems that were already straining the AI hardware boom: Nvidia had become the industry's largest startup investor, and its best chips had become the most contested export in the world. Both roles now carry a bill, and the company's new caretaker has to decide who pays it.

## The exit, in brief

Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and turned it into the defining hardware company of the AI era. Its data-centre accelerators became the scarce input for every serious model lab, and its CUDA software stack became the moat around them. The stock market treated the company as a proxy for the entire artificial-intelligence trade.

Kress, who has been Nvidia's finance chief since 2018, inherits a business with enormous momentum and an unusually public leadership vacuum. She is not a stranger to the capital markets or to the company's customers. She is, however, a different kind of operator: more conservative with money, less inclined to make billion-dollar bets on the strength of a personal relationship.

That distinction matters because Nvidia's strategy has not only been about selling chips. It has been about financing the companies that buy them.

## The funding machine

According to PitchBook data cited by TechCrunch, Nvidia participated in 105 funding rounds this year, frequently as the largest investor. Its balance sheet reached into nearly every layer of the AI stack: neoclouds such as CoreWeave and Crusoe, inference providers, open-weight model builders, and the tooling layer around them. It agreed to buy Hugging Face for roughly $13 billion, a deal expected to close in 2027, and holds positions in Mistral AI, Lambda, and Black Forest Labs.

Some checks were extraordinary even by AI-cycle standards. Nvidia reportedly wrote an $800 million cheque into Reflection AI's $2 billion Series B before the startup had meaningful revenue.

This was not charity. Investing in customers and suppliers helped lock them into Nvidia hardware. A startup that takes Nvidia money builds on CUDA, buys Nvidia accelerators, and designs its inference economics around Nvidia's roadmap. Capital and silicon became a single product, sold by a single salesman.

The risk in that arrangement was always concentration. Investors and founders are now openly asking what happens when the salesman leaves. One neocloud founder told TechCrunch the news felt "like a bomb went off", and venture investors expect Kress to tighten the chequebook. The Information reported that Nvidia's dealmaking binge faces a reckoning under her interim leadership.

If Nvidia pulls back, the first casualty is not the frontier labs. It is the long tail of infrastructure startups that relied on Nvidia to anchor otherwise unfinanceable rounds. Hyperscalers and rival chipmakers may eventually fill some of the gap, but they will do so on their own terms, and they will expect their own silicon to benefit.

## The export-control problem

The second bill is regulatory, and it is older than the scandal.

This week, the nonprofit research group Unemployment Labs published an analysis of the internal structure of open-weight models released by DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, and Ant Group. The researcher Will DePue argued that the weights of several leading Chinese models, including Moonshot's Kimi K3 and DeepSeek's V4 line, carry fingerprints consistent with training on Nvidia's Blackwell chips.

Blackwell is exactly the generation that US export controls prohibit shipping to China or to Chinese-owned firms. The White House technology adviser, Michael Kratsios, said earlier this year that Moonshot had used restricted Nvidia chips, citing intelligence sources. Bloomberg also reported on Friday that a company linked to Alice Huang was involved in moving banned Nvidia chips to a data centre in Shanghai.

The scale of the grey market is not small. Epoch AI estimates that around two million advanced chips entered China in 2024 and 2025 through schemes designed to obscure their final destination, including about 275,000 Nvidia accelerators imported to Malaysia alone.

There is an important caveat. Weight forensics can suggest which hardware a model was trained on, but it cannot prove where that hardware was installed or who owned it. The chips could have sat in an offshore data centre, a route Chinese firms have used extensively and one that has helped turn Malaysia into a data-centre hub. Training on Blackwell is not automatically a violation of the letter of the rules.

It is, however, a demonstration that the rules are aimed at the wrong target. Export controls restrict who can buy a chip and where it can be installed. They do not reliably restrict who can use it. A Chinese lab that rents time in a Malaysian facility gets much of the benefit of the hardware without ever taking delivery of it.

For Nvidia, that is both a commercial opportunity and a legal exposure. Huang spent years arguing that locking Nvidia out of China would hurt American competitiveness and hand the market to domestic alternatives. The investigation into Megaspeed, and the short-seller report that preceded it, reframed that argument as a compliance question. A caretaker CEO has little appetite for relitigating it.

## Why this is a hardware story

It is easy to read the Nvidia turmoil as a Silicon Valley personality story. The hardware implications are more durable.

The AI build-out has been financed on the assumption that Nvidia's roadmap, balance sheet, and customer relationships would remain stable. That assumption is now doing less work. Three pressure points stand out:

| Pressure point | What changes |
| --- | --- |
| Startup financing | Nvidia's chequebook slows, pushing neoclouds and inference providers toward hyperscalers or rival chipmakers |
| Product cadence | A caretaker CEO is less likely to make aggressive roadmap bets or absorb short-term margin pain |
| Export enforcement | Stricter end-use monitoring would raise costs and slow shipments, even to permitted buyers |

None of these kills the AI hardware trade. Demand for compute still exceeds supply, and Nvidia's software ecosystem remains years ahead of its rivals. But the company's valuation has rested on more than demand. It has rested on the belief that Nvidia can shape the market it sells into. That belief was inseparable from Huang.

The beneficiaries are already positioning. Google announced two new specialised AI chips last week, a reminder that the hyperscalers have both the motive and the engineering capacity to reduce their dependence on one supplier. AMD and the custom-silicon teams inside Amazon and Microsoft have the same incentive. Every quarter of Nvidia uncertainty is a quarter of opportunity for someone else.

## What to watch

Four signals will tell us whether this is a leadership transition or a strategic inflection point.

1. **Kress's first capital decisions.** Watch whether Nvidia honours its announced commitments and how it treats the Hugging Face acquisition timeline. A pause would be read as a retreat.
2. **The next earnings call.** Data-centre guidance and backlog matter, but so does commentary on customer concentration and export compliance. Expect analysts to press on both.
3. **Enforcement in Southeast Asia.** Malaysia and Singapore have become the chokepoints for diverted hardware. New licensing or audit requirements would change the economics of the grey market quickly.
4. **The successor.** A permanent CEO from inside the chip business signals continuity. An outsider with a finance or operations background signals a more disciplined, less expansionary Nvidia.

There is also a broader lesson for the industry. The AI boom has been built on a remarkable concentration of capability: one company's chips, one company's software stack, and, until this week, one company's founder setting the pace. That concentration made the boom possible. It also means a single resignation can reprice the entire supply chain.

## The bottom line

Huang's departure is not the end of Nvidia. The company still sells the most important hardware in the industry, and its order book is measured in years. But the ecosystem around it was financed by a man who treated investing, selling, and evangelising as one job. Kress inherits the company, not the role.

For buyers of AI hardware, the practical consequence is straightforward: diversify earlier than feels comfortable. For Nvidia's competitors, the window is open. For Nvidia itself, the bill for building an entire market on one balance sheet and one personality has finally arrived.

The next CEO will not be able to pay it with another keynote.
